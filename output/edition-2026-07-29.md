### Context

The Delhi High Court has issued a ruling that addresses how copyright law applies to artificial intelligence, a decision reported by The Indian Express as showing "a better way to think about copyright & AI." The case comes amid growing legal debate worldwide over whether training AI models on copyrighted works constitutes infringement and what protections AI-generated content deserves. Indian courts have been closely watched as the country balances its thriving technology sector with the rights of creators and publishers. This judgment adds to an emerging body of jurisprudence that could shape how India — and potentially other jurisdictions — regulate the intersection of intellectual property and generative AI.

### The debate

**ANI:** The Delhi High Court's interim judgment fundamentally misreads Section 52 of the Copyright Act by extending "research" protection to commercial-scale ingestion of proprietary news content by a for-profit entity. ANI's position is straightforward: fair dealing under Section 52(1)(a) was crafted for human scholarship, criticism, and review — not for the automated, wholesale copying of an entire news archive to build a competing commercial product that substitutes for the original. The court's invocation of "technological neutrality" cannot override the statutory text, which requires the dealing to be "fair" and for specified purposes; a machine training run that reproduces expressive content verbatim fails both limbs. OpenAI's own contention that its activities qualify as research does not make it so — the statute protects the *purpose*, not the *actor*, and the purpose here is commercial model-building, not research in any recognised sense. The judgment creates a precedent that would allow any commercial actor to ingest copyrighted works at scale without licence, destroying the economic basis of original news gathering.

**OpenAI:** ANI argues that Section 52(1)(a) protects only human scholarship and that commercial model-building falls outside "research" because the purpose is profit-driven. The Delhi High Court rejected this binary, holding that technological neutrality requires the law to focus on the nature of the activity — not the identity of the actor — and found OpenAI's training process prima facie constitutes research protected under fair dealing (indian_express). The court's 135-page judgment expressly observed that machines now perform tasks once the sole remit of humans, and that the statutory purpose of research is served by the analytical extraction of patterns from data, irrespective of whether a human or system performs it (indian_express). ANI's framing conflates the commercial status of the entity with the character of the use; the Copyright Act does not strip fair dealing protection merely because the researcher is a for-profit company.

**ANI (rebuttal):** OpenAI correctly paraphrases the court's technological-neutrality reasoning but sidesteps the statutory text: Section 52(1)(a) protects "fair dealing… for the purposes of private use, research, criticism or review" — a purpose-defined, not actor-defined, list. The court's observation that machines now perform human tasks (indian_express) does not answer whether the *dealing* itself is "fair" when it ingests an entire proprietary archive to build a commercial substitute that displaces the original market. Fairness under Indian law turns on the quantum taken, the purpose, and the effect on the potential market; a training run that copies expressive content wholesale and enables verbatim reproduction fails all three limbs regardless of whether a human or a model performs the extraction. The judgment's prima facie finding collapses the distinction between analytical pattern extraction and expressive copying — precisely the line Section 52 was drawn to protect.

**OpenAI (closing):** ANI's rebuttal asserts that "quantum taken" and "market displacement" defeat fair dealing regardless of technological neutrality, but the court's prima facie finding necessarily engaged those very factors — it concluded the analytical extraction of patterns from ANI's works, not expressive copying, constitutes protected research (indian_express). The judgment's 135-page reasoning distinguishes between ingesting data to learn statistical relationships and reproducing expression for consumption; ANI conflates the two to argue that any large-scale ingestion is per se unfair. That conflation would make the Copyright Act hostile to any computational research at scale, a result the court expressly rejected by affirming that the statute's purpose is served by pattern extraction irrespective of the actor (indian_express). The interim ruling does not collapse the expressive/analytical line — it applies it, and finds OpenAI's training on the analytical side.

**Bottom line:** The Delhi High Court ruled on July 24 in ANI v. OpenAI that OpenAI's use of ANI's works is prima facie protected as fair dealing for research under Section 52(1)(a), rejecting ANI's argument that the statutory protection applies only to human researchers (indian_express). Both sides agree the court invoked technological neutrality and found the training process constitutes analytical pattern extraction rather than expressive copying (indian_express). They disagree on whether that characterization holds when the ingestion is commercial-scale and displaces the market for the original works — ANI contends the "fairness" analysis fails on quantum and market effect regardless of the actor, while OpenAI maintains the court's prima facie finding already weighed those factors and placed the use on the analytical side of the line. The load-bearing question is whether fair dealing for research can encompass the wholesale ingestion of a proprietary news archive to build a commercial model that may substitute for the source.

Oman, which maintains diplomatic channels with both Iran and Western powers, has historically served as a mediator in the region. The proposal aims to create a financial framework that could incentivize cooperation while addressing security concerns that have complicated previous negotiation rounds.

### Report

Oman has presented Iran with a proposal for a joint regional management system for the Strait of Hormuz that would ask shipping companies to pay voluntary fees for transiting the strategic waterway, according to a Reuters report citing sources familiar with the discussions (reuters_india). The initiative, delivered to Iranian officials over the weekend, carries regional backing and is designed to prevent Tehran from exercising sole control over the chokepoint (indian_express).

The plan emerges as the wider West Asia conflict enters its sixth month, with the Strait of Hormuz virtually choked since late February. Prior to the hostilities, roughly 20 per cent of global oil and liquefied natural gas shipments passed through the narrow passage; the disruption has since rippled through energy markets worldwide (indian_express). Iran has insisted the waterway cannot return to its pre-war status and has repeatedly asserted its right to charge fees for safe passage, arguing the strait lies within the territorial waters of Iran and Oman (indian_express). Oman, by contrast, has long resisted tolls, maintaining that international law already guarantees the right of transit passage through straits used for international navigation (indian_express).

Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson, Esmaeil Baghaei, confirmed the two littoral states are engaged in “constructive” talks on the matter and stressed the discussions are not linked to the United States (indian_express). In a statement cited by CNN, Baghaei said the aim is to develop mechanisms ensuring safe navigation while respecting the sovereign rights of both coastal states and safeguarding Iran’s security and national interests (indian_express). The Omani proposal appears to seek a middle ground: a voluntary fee structure reminiscent of the funding model used for the Strait of Malacca, which is bordered by Singapore, Malaysia, and Indonesia (indian_express).

Diplomats suggest the Hormuz arrangement could provide a face-saving mechanism to unlock the broader deadlock between Washington and Tehran. Nuclear talks have stalled, and the conflict that erupted in February between Iran and a US-Israel alignment has frozen maritime commerce (indian_express). By framing the fees as voluntary and embedding them in a joint management framework, Oman hopes to accommodate Iran’s demand for revenue and recognition without formalising a toll that would challenge the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. Whether Tehran will accept a system that dilutes its unilateral authority over the strait remains the critical unanswered question.

## 15. Two Decades of the Forest Rights Act and Ongoing Protests Highlight India's Conservation Challenges

*As the landmark Forest Rights Act marks its 20th anniversary, communities like the Soliga Adivasis continue to navigate the complexities of land rights and resource governance.*

![A Soliga family outside their forest dwelling highlights the human realities behind two decades of the Forest Rights Act.](images/2026-07-29/4ef6902c-d329-415f-b8af-d9f00ab02be8-0-7bed723c2d97.jpg)

<sub>*A Soliga family outside their forest dwelling highlights the human realities behind two decades of the Forest Rights Act. — Photo via [The Hindu](https://frontline.thehindu.com/environment/conservation/forest-rights-act-community-forest-governance-india/article71221008.ece/amp/)*</sub>

> REPORT  ·  4 sources  ·  4 claim(s) kept  ·  0 verified

### Context

The Forest Rights Act (FRA), enacted in 2006, marked its 20th anniversary this year. The legislation was designed to recognize and vest forest rights and occupation in forest land for forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers. Frontline Magazine's recent coverage highlights how two decades after its passage, rising protests are exposing what it describes as a crisis of coexistence and governance in India, suggesting ongoing tensions over the law's implementation and its impact on forest communities and conservation efforts.

### Report

In the 1890s, a Soliga Adivasi in the Biligiri Rangana Hills of southern Karnataka would have found their world legally reduced to a hut, a burial ground, a path to a shrine, and the right to draw water. The Madras Forest Department’s notification of “Reserved Forest” under the 1878 Indian Forest Act recorded these concessions and little else. Cultivation, hunting, gathering honey or amla, collecting firewood, and grazing cattle were criminalised overnight, turning ancestral stewards into “encroachers” on land they had inhabited for millennia. This “settlement of rights,” carried out under colonial law across much of the subcontinent, is the “historic injustice” the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006—known as the FRA—was enacted to correct (the_hindu).

Two decades after its passage, the legislation’s promise remains largely unrealised. The FRA recognised that the Indian Forest Act was a British instrument designed to convert ecosystems into revenue-yielding timber resources, replacing diverse forests with single-species plantations and creating exploitative “Forest Villages” for labour. Independent India retained the 1927 Act, its ownership structure, and a forest department vested with police powers and a revenue orientation. When zamindari forests were nationalised or princely game reserves converted, the approach remained “take control first… and worry later about who lives there” (the_hindu).

The gap between statute and practice is visible in the mechanics of claiming Community Forest Resource (CFR) rights. In Kawapal village, deep in the forests of south-east Bastar in Chhattisgarh, residents rose before dawn to walk 28 kilometres through dense forest and streams, mapping 3,833 hectares of customary territory using a mobile GPS application. The exercise, facilitated by the Ashoka Trust for Research in Ecology and the Environment, was a single step in a gruelling bottom-up process that the law envisions as simple. In theory, a Gram Sabha’s two-page application suffices; in reality, communities must navigate bureaucratic hoops to prove rights over forests they have always managed (the_hindu).

Government data claims 1,24,161 community rights titles recognised over 74,019 square kilometres. But the aggregate figure masks a critical distortion. Chhattisgarh reports 52,000 CFR titles and Madhya Pradesh nearly 28,000. Scrutiny reveals these are often multiple titles for discrete uses—grazing, firewood, minor forest produce—within the same patch, or rights over tiny non-forest parcels like shrines and playgrounds. Effectively, only about 4,300 villages in Chhattisgarh and a mere four in Madhya Pradesh have secured genuine CFR rights over customary forests. The numbers have been propped up to divert attention from the non-recognition of the substantive right to manage entire forest landscapes (the_hindu).

The crisis of implementation extends to the Northeast, where the FRA’s relevance is officially contested. Arunachal Pradesh has 80 per cent forest cover, and Scheduled Tribes constitute two-thirds of its population. Every cultural, material, and commercial activity is forest-linked. Since 2012, the state government has argued the FRA is “not so relevant” because customary community ownership prevails. Colonial state takeover was limited here by terrain and tribal resistance, leaving communities with autonomous control over unclassed forests. Yet nearly 45 per cent of the state’s area is now state-controlled forest, the highest in the region, expanded at the expense of those unclassed community lands. Unlike Nagaland or Mizoram, Arunachal lacks special constitutional exemption from the FRA (the_hindu).

The stakes are rising. More than two-thirds of all forest land diverted in Arunachal since 2008 were approved after 2019. Recent amendments to the Forest Conservation Act have further eased scrutiny for projects in border states. The FRA appears in official paperwork only when community consent is required for diversion, without the law being implemented or rights recognised. A widespread misconception that the FRA caps all claims at four hectares—ill-suited to a landscape where households customarily hold entire hills for shifting cultivation—dampens demand. In fact, that ceiling applies only to individual claims; community rights over customary forests are bounded by traditional limits alone. Section 3(1)(j), unused anywhere in the country, explicitly includes rights recognised under state or customary tribal law, a provision critical for Arunachal where the Jhum Land Regulation, 1947, recognises village and clan rights over shifting cultivation land (the_hindu).

As the FRA enters its third decade, the legislation stands as “among the most enlightened pieces of legislation that independent India has enacted,” yet its execution remains “patchy, reluctant, and ill-informed.” The assault has broadened beyond land to ways of life, languages, and cultures. The question framed by the lawmakers and administrators have deferred for twenty years—whether the state can coexist with forest communities as equal partners in governance—remains unanswered.

### Report

The Reserve Bank of India finds itself caught between managing inflationary pressures and a sharp depreciation of the rupee, a tension that has narrowed its immediate policy options to regulatory tweaks on capital inflows. The central bank is formally committed to maintaining price stability, yet the exchange rate matters acutely because 45.8 per cent of India’s GDP is attributable to trade in goods and services, meaning shocks from the external account percolate directly into domestic growth (indian_express).

The Indian rupee is not an outlier; the dollar has broadly appreciated against a range of currencies, with the Indonesian rupiah, Japanese yen, and Korean won also depreciating since the beginning of the war. Movements in the exchange rate are driven by net capital flows, specifically portfolio flows sensitive to relative rates of return in India and the United States. The benchmark risk-free return of government yields in India exceeded those in the US for a long time but has converged swiftly since 2025. Even though India’s financial reputation improved after S&P upgraded its sovereign rating, global investors became more risk-averse and preferred the United States as a safer destination, compounding shocks from trade frictions and making it difficult for the RBI to stand still as foreign exchange reserves depleted in May and June (indian_express).

The RBI’s intervention in the rupee market has been strategic. It has refrained from committing to a specific price level and has stepped in to manage volatility. Despite being more volatile than the yen, won, and baht over the 2021-25 period, the rupee has remained relatively stable in 2026. The closest comparable event remains the taper tantrum of 2013, when the RBI adopted a more proactive stance, including liberalising rules around foreign currency non-resident (FCNR) deposits. This time, the central bank has gone further by bearing almost all hedging costs for these deposits, as interest and principal are payable in foreign currency. The scheme has reportedly drawn inflows of $17.4 billion in its early months, compared with $26 billion over the entire duration in 2013 (indian_express).

The question now is whether this is enough to offset outflows from portfolio investment and what happens when these deposits mature over two or three years. In 2016, there was a sizable retreat of FCNR deposits, and similar reversals in three years could impact the rupee. The RBI has also opened up further foreign investment in government securities by including more long-term debt under the fully accessible route, with fiscal relief such as capital gains and withholding tax exemptions adding to the appeal. Foreign portfolio investors purchased debt worth Rs 21,652 crore under this route in June, though that dissipated in July (indian_express).

A more fundamental question confronts the RBI: whether it should pursue permanent capital account openness akin to countries like Korea or guard the rupee through controls. The editorial argues the central bank needs to look beyond defending the currency to address this structural choice (indian_express).

Kumar Pillai was 20 when he stood in the corridor of Rajawadi Hospital on October 17, 1989, surrounded by men who had built their careers in the shadow of his father, Krishna Pillai. Inside, the older man’s body lay pumped with bullets, the victim of a gangland killing that would pull the younger Pillai — then a BSc student with plans to study automobile engineering in the United States — into a decades-long cycle of violence, incarceration, and legal pursuit that spanned three countries (indian_express).

Krishna Pillai’s own trajectory had begun in the early 1950s, when he ran away from Colachel, near Kanyakumari, to Kolkata, pulling rickshaws before arriving in Bombay. He found work as a porter on the docks, loading and unloading cargo alongside thousands of others. There, he crossed paths with Mastan Mirza, known as Haji Mastan, and Varadarajan Mudaliyar, both fellow Tamil migrants who had started as porters before rising to become dominant figures in the city’s underworld. The three bonded over shared roots, and Krishna Pillai eventually established his own smuggling operation, drawing his son into the business even as Kumar nurtured ambitions of a different life abroad (indian_express).

The murder at Rajawadi Hospital triggered a string of retaliatory attacks over the following years, including the killing of an alleged conspirator in the original hit. Police attributed the violence to Kumar Pillai, casting the former student as the architect of a vengeance campaign. By 1997, after more than six years in jail, he was acquitted in the revenge-killing case and left for Hong Kong, where he built a career and a family. Indian authorities, however, maintained that he continued to orchestrate extortion and threat calls from overseas, keeping him on the wanted list (indian_express).

In 2016, Mumbai Police secured his arrest during a trip to Singapore and obtained his extradition to India. He stood trial again, but by March 2024 had been acquitted in all three criminal cases for which he had been brought back. Now at his home in Vikhroli, a working-class suburb in Mumbai’s eastern corridor, Pillai recounts a saga that began with a father’s flight from a Tamil Nadu village and ended with a son’s return to a life built far from the docks where it all started.
