This impasse reflects broader tensions within the organization over the future direction of global trade rules and the balance between inclusive multilateralism and smaller-group negotiations.

### The debate

**India (Commerce Ministry delegation):** India's position is grounded in the WTO's founding principle: consensus-based, multilateral rule-making where all 164 members have equal voice and veto. Plurilateral agreements on e-commerce and investment, negotiated by self-selected groups and then presented as _faits accomplis_, bypass that architecture and effectively impose rules on developing countries without their consent — rules that often constrain digital policy space and investment regulation precisely when India needs them for development. (indian_express)

The Commerce Secretary's delegation in Geneva detailed India's extensive unilateral reforms — tariff rationalisation, PLI schemes for manufacturing depth, and quality control orders for consumer safety — demonstrating that India is delivering predictability and market access through transparent, WTO-consistent domestic action, not through exclusive clubs. (indian_express, google_news_india)

The charge that India is "holding up reform" inverts the problem: the reform proposals being pushed rely on plurilaterals to sidestep the consensus deficit on issues where developing members have legitimate objections. India reaffirmed its commitment to an open, transparent, WTO-consistent regime; the question is whether the WTO remains a forum for negotiated multilateral outcomes or becomes a platform for coalition-of-the-willing rule-setting. (indian_express, google_news_india)

**WTO members (EU, Canada, UK, Cambodia, Gambia):** The Indian delegation frames plurilaterals as an imposition, but the WTO's own architecture — Article II:3 of the Marrakesh Agreement — explicitly provides for plurilateral agreements that bind only those members who accept them; they are not "faits accomplis" but a negotiated, consensus-enabled pathway within the treaty. (indian_express)

The reform paralysis India defends is the very reason plurilaterals have "begun featuring prominently in various WTO reform-related proposals" — because the consensus rule has blocked meaningful rule updates for decades, and a coalition of the willing including the EU, Canada, UK, Cambodia and Gambia is now using the WTO's own legal tools to move forward on e-commerce and investment. (indian_express)

India's unilateral reforms are welcome, but they do not substitute for binding, enforceable market-access commitments; members at the Trade Policy Review specifically questioned the PLI scheme, tariffs and QCO regime precisely because those measures affect market access and trade predictability in ways that voluntary domestic action cannot lock in. (google_news_india)

**India (Commerce Ministry delegation) (rebuttal):** The other side invokes Article II:3 to legitimise plurilaterals, but that provision was designed for narrow sectoral codes, not economy-wide rule-setting on e-commerce and investment that effectively rewrites the multilateral bargain for non-participants through regulatory spillover and most-favoured-nation erosion. (indian_express)

### Report

In the wake of the 1905 Partition of Bengal, the Swadeshi movement gathered unprecedented momentum. As crowds shouted “Vande Mataram” and called for the boycott of British goods, another label quietly began to acquire new political meaning in Indian bazaars: ‘Made in Germany’ (indian_express).

While the Swadeshi movement opened opportunities for several non-British industrial powers, German products occupied a distinctive place in Indian markets. This reflected both the growing commercial rivalry between Britain and Germany and Germany’s increasingly hostile political relationship with Britain, which would culminate in the First World War (indian_express). “The idea of the enemy’s enemy being one’s friend takes off significantly, and the Swadeshi activists actively collaborate with the Germans to avoid the British,” historian Christina Lubinski told indianexpress.com (indian_express).

No industry benefited more from this political climate than German synthetic dyes. By the early 20th century, firms such as BASF, Bayer, and Hoechst had established a near-monopoly over the global synthetic dye industry, with India emerging as one of their most important overseas markets (indian_express). The prominence of dyes went far beyond the textile trade. Synthetic dyes represented one of the world’s earliest science-intensive industries, bringing together laboratory research, industrial chemistry and manufacturing on an unprecedented scale. Their production required sophisticated scientific knowledge that few countries possessed (indian_express).

For Indian textile manufacturers, German dyes offered consistency, quality and a range of colours that competitors struggled to match. For German companies, India became an indispensable market (indian_express). Yet Germany never ruled India. Unlike Britain, it possessed no formal colonial authority on the subcontinent. Its influence instead flowed through trade, technology, and commercial networks that developed within the framework of the British Empire (indian_express). “But informally there is a form of colonialism baked into these relationships. There was also a clear power imbalance that the Germans were actively exploiting,” Lubinski argues (indian_express).

Germany’s own colonial empire was both late and short-lived. It began only in 1884 and ended abruptly after the First World War, leaving German firms to navigate Indian markets without the protection of a colonial state (indian_express).
