### Context

The Supreme Court of India is scrutinizing the appointment of a minister in Bihar who is not an elected member of the state legislature. The court's inquiry, framed by the question "How are you keeping unelected minister?", signals a constitutional examination of the provisions allowing non-legislators to serve in the council of ministers. This legal challenge addresses the validity and duration of such appointments under the Indian Constitution.

### Report

The Supreme Court on Thursday questioned how the Bihar government could retain a minister who has remained unelected beyond the six-month constitutional limit, asking the state to explain its position. A three-judge bench presided over by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant was hearing a request to fix a date for a plea challenging the appointment of Deepak Prakash as Panchayati Raj Minister for a second time without his having been elected to the state legislature (indian_express).

“It is a pure legal issue. How are you retaining a minister beyond 6 months when he is not elected?” CJI Kant asked the counsel appearing for the Bihar Government (indian_express). Article 164(4) of the Constitution permits a non-member to serve as a minister for six months, provided they secure election to the legislature within that period (indian_express).

The petitioner, Rakesh Kumar Singh, argued that Prakash was first sworn in on November 20, 2025, in the then Nitish Kumar government despite not being a member of the Bihar Legislative Assembly or the Legislative Council (indian_express). After a new council of ministers was formed following Kumar’s departure and Samrat Choudhary’s elevation as Chief Minister, Prakash was reappointed to the same portfolio on May 7, 2026 (indian_express). Singh contended that the constitutional provision offers a one-time opportunity that cannot be reset with every change of government, and that Prakash was required to get elected between November 20, 2025, and May 20, 2026 (indian_express).

During the hearing, counsel for Singh noted that the state had sought time on July 15 to file a counter-affidavit but had not done so. “Seven months and 22 days have passed, and he is still continuing as the minister,” he said (indian_express). CJI Kant responded that the court would not grant further time regardless of whether a counter-affidavit is filed, and fixed the next hearing for August 4 (indian_express).

The plea argues that the reappointment circumvents constitutional safeguards and undermines representative democracy, parliamentary accountability, and collective responsibility (indian_express). It seeks a declaration that Prakash’s reappointment and continuance in office are unconstitutional, illegal, and void, along with a restraint on his exercise of ministerial powers (indian_express).
